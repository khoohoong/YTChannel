## COLD OPEN (0:00–0:15)

There is a place where the oldest stories on Earth still breathe. Where temples rise from the jungle like the bones of forgotten gods. Where the sea itself was once a highway — and tiny humans, no taller than a child, walked the earth beside us. This is Southeast Asia. And its true origin… is stranger than you think.

## SEGMENT 1 — THE HOBBITS OF FLORES (0:15–1:40)

In 2003, on the remote Indonesian island of Flores, a team of archaeologists made a discovery that shook the foundations of everything we thought we knew about human evolution.

Deep inside a limestone cave called Liang Bua, they found a skeleton. It was complete, well-preserved, and clearly human. But something was deeply wrong. The skull was no larger than a grapefruit. The body stood barely one meter tall. This was not a child. It was a fully grown adult.

They named her LB1. The press called her the Hobbit. Science called her Homo floresiensis — a species of human being that had never been seen before. The remains are now dated to between sixty thousand and one hundred thousand years old, and stone tools on the island hint that her kind may have walked here for hundreds of thousands of years before that.

How did she get so small? On islands, large animals shrink and small animals grow — a phenomenon called island dwarfism. Flores had dwarf elephants too, tiny Stegodons the Hobbits hunted with simple stone tools. Isolated from the rest of the world, an entire branch of humanity evolved in miniature — and thrived.

But here is the part that haunts researchers. The Hobbits disappeared from Flores around fifty thousand years ago — at almost exactly the moment modern humans were spreading through Southeast Asia for the first time. Was it coincidence? Or did our arrival — through competition, disease, or something darker — help drive them to extinction? Recent studies even point to a drying climate closing in around them in their final millennia.

The caves of Flores hold no certain answer. Only silence.

## SEGMENT 2 — THE AUSTRONESIAN EXPANSION (1:40–3:10)

But long before science came to these islands, someone else did. And they came by sea.

Beginning around five thousand years ago, a people we now call the Austronesians launched one of the most extraordinary journeys in human history. From the island of Taiwan, their descendants pushed out into the open ocean roughly four thousand years ago, in double-hulled outrigger canoes — vessels of astonishing engineering — sailing into the unknown.

They had no compass. No charts. No metal tools. What they had was the sky. Austronesian navigators read the stars like a map. They tracked the movement of ocean swells against the hull. They watched the flight patterns of birds and the colour of clouds reflected off distant lagoons. This was not luck. This was science — passed down through generations in song and memory.

And it worked. Over the course of three thousand years, they settled every inhabitable island in the Pacific and Indian Oceans — from Madagascar off the coast of Africa to Rapa Nui, Easter Island, the most remote inhabited place on Earth. It was the single largest maritime migration in human history, spanning more than half the planet.

In Southeast Asia, they became the ancestors of the Malay, the Javanese, the Filipino, the Polynesian peoples. They brought with them rice cultivation, domesticated animals, pottery, and a family of languages — the Austronesian language family — more than a thousand tongues still spoken by nearly four hundred million people today.

They did not conquer this region. They became it. Every nation in maritime Southeast Asia carries their legacy in its blood, its language, and its rice fields.

## SEGMENT 3 — THE NAGA AND THE SPIRIT WORLD (3:10–4:40)

But the Austronesians were not sailing into an empty world. The rivers and jungles of mainland Southeast Asia were already home to ancient civilisations — and ancient beliefs.

Chief among them was the Naga.

In the mythology of Southeast Asia, the Naga is a serpent of immense power. It dwells in rivers, lakes, and underground waterways. It controls the rains, guards hidden treasures, and serves as a bridge between the mortal world and the realm of the gods. In some traditions, the Naga is benevolent — a protector. In others, it is a force of terrifying destruction.

The Naga appears in the oldest Hindu and Buddhist texts brought to the region by Indian traders. But scholars now believe the serpent myth is far older than that — indigenous to Southeast Asia itself, rooted in the reverence these river-dwelling civilisations held for the water that gave them life.

You can still see the Naga everywhere. Carved into the stone balustrades of Angkor. Winding along the staircases of Thai and Lao temples. Painted on the prows of royal barges. In the Mekong River, locals still report seeing the Naga's fireballs — mysterious lights that rise from the water each autumn. Science has no agreed explanation. The people of the river need none. They have always known what lives below.

The Naga is not a relic. It is alive — coiled through the spiritual heart of a continent.

## SEGMENT 4 — ANGKOR: THE GREATEST CITY ON EARTH (4:40–6:10)

And it was from this world — this fusion of ocean voyagers and river gods, of trade and faith and ambition — that one of the greatest civilisations in human history emerged.

Angkor.

At its height between the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, the Khmer Empire ruled most of mainland Southeast Asia from a capital that dwarfed anything in medieval Europe. Greater Angkor sprawled across nearly three thousand square kilometres and was home to somewhere between seven hundred thousand and nine hundred thousand people — the largest city of the entire pre-industrial world. When Angkor Wat was rising, London held perhaps twenty thousand souls. Angkor's engineers built a water management system of reservoirs, canals, and barays so vast that its imprint can still be traced from the air today. Its temples numbered over a thousand.

And at its centre stood Angkor Wat. The largest religious monument ever built. Begun under King Suryavarman the Second, it was a stone mountain dedicated to the Hindu god Vishnu, its walls covered in kilometres of bas-relief carvings depicting gods, armies, dancers, and the churning of the cosmic ocean. It took around thirty years and thousands of labourers to raise. Nine hundred years later, it still stands.

But Angkor did not last. A changing climate brought drought. Wars with neighbouring kingdoms drained the treasury. The great hydraulic system that fed the city began to fail. By the fifteenth century, the capital was abandoned. The jungle moved in — slowly, silently, unstoppably — swallowing temples whole, splitting stone with roots, burying a million lives under a canopy of green.

When French explorers stumbled upon Angkor in the nineteenth century, they could not believe that the people living in its shadow had built it. They were wrong. The descendants of Angkor never left. They simply let the forest take back what the forest had given.

## OUTRO (6:10–6:25)

From the tiny bones of Flores to the star roads of the Austronesians. From the serpent beneath the river to the towers above the jungle. Southeast Asia's true origin is not one story. It is many — ancient, layered, and still unfolding.

And we have barely scratched the surface.
