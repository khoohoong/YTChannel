## COLD OPEN (hook)

Picture the warm blue sea south of Asia. Thousands of green islands. Palm trees, white beaches, coral reefs. It looks like paradise, right?

But hidden under that water is one of the biggest secrets on Earth. A whole lost land — bigger than you can imagine — now sitting at the bottom of the sea.

This is Southeast Asia. And its beginning is way stranger, and way cooler, than you were ever told.

Tiny humans the size of a kid. The oldest story ever painted on a wall. Sailors who crossed half the planet without a single map. And a city so huge that the jungle swallowed it whole.

Ready? Let's go back — way, way back.

## SEGMENT 1 — THE LOST LAND UNDER THE SEA

Let's start with that lost land.

About twenty thousand years ago, the Earth was much colder. This was the Ice Age. So much of the world's water was frozen into giant sheets of ice that the oceans actually dropped — by more than a hundred meters. That's taller than a thirty-story building.

And when the sea dropped, something amazing happened. Where you see separate islands today — like Sumatra, Borneo, and Java — there was once one giant piece of dry land. Scientists call it Sundaland.

It was huge. The land was about twice the size it is now. Wide green plains. Rivers you could follow for hundreds of miles. Forests full of animals. And people — real people — lived there, hunting and fishing, walking to places that today you could only reach by boat.

But then the Ice Age ended. The world warmed up. The giant ice sheets melted, and all that water came flooding back into the sea.

Slowly, over thousands of years, the ocean rose… and rose. The plains flooded. The rivers vanished. And the people living there had to move to higher and higher ground — the hills that are now islands.

Little by little, between about fourteen thousand and seven thousand years ago, the sea swallowed Sundaland whole. It's still down there today, under the warm, shallow water. A drowned world — sitting right at the start of Southeast Asia's story.

## SEGMENT 2 — THE OLDEST STORY ON EARTH

Now let's talk about a cave. And the oldest story that humans ever told.

On an island called Sulawesi, in Indonesia, there are caves with paintings on the walls. For a long time, people believed the oldest art in the whole world was in Europe.

They were wrong.

In 2024, scientists carefully studied a painting deep inside one of these Sulawesi caves. Using a clever way of measuring the rock around it, they figured out how old the painting really was. And the answer stunned everyone.

The painting is at least fifty-one thousand years old.

Fifty-one thousand! That makes it the oldest known artwork on the entire planet. But here's the part that gives me goosebumps. It isn't just a doodle. It shows a wild pig standing still — and three little human-like figures reaching toward it, as if they are hunting it, or telling a story about it.

Think about that. It's not a picture of just one thing. It's a scene. Something is happening in it.

That makes it the oldest known story ever told by human hands — older than any book, any letter, any word ever written down.

Fifty-one thousand years ago, someone stood in that cave, dipped their hand in red paint, and basically said: let me tell you what happened. And somehow, we can still read it today.

## SEGMENT 3 — THE TINY PEOPLE OF FLORES

Next stop: another Indonesian island, called Flores. And this one holds one of the strangest discoveries in all of science.

In 2003, a team of archaeologists was digging inside a deep cave called Liang Bua. In the dirt, they found a skeleton. It looked human — but it was tiny. A fully grown adult that stood only about one meter tall. That's shorter than a lot of ten-year-olds. And the head? The skull was only about the size of a grapefruit.

At first, some people thought maybe it was just one unusual person. But then they found more of them. This wasn't a single strange skeleton. It was a whole kind of human — a species nobody had ever seen before.

Scientists named them Homo floresiensis. Everybody else just called them… the Hobbits.

So how could humans end up so small? Here comes the cool science. When animals live on an island for a very long time, something odd can happen. Big animals often shrink over many generations, because a smaller body needs less food to survive.

And Flores proves it. The very same island had dwarf elephants — elephants shrunk down almost as small as a cow! The Hobbits hunted them using simple stone tools.

For hundreds of thousands of years, these little people lived on their island, in their own tiny world. And then, around fifty thousand years ago, they vanished. Scientists think the weather on the island slowly changed and got drier, until the animals they depended on became harder and harder to find.

And here's the strangest part of all. People like us — modern humans — didn't reach that cave until a few thousand years *after* the Hobbits were already gone. We showed up… just a little too late to ever meet them.

## SEGMENT 4 — THE GREATEST SEA JOURNEY EVER

Now for my favorite part. The greatest adventure story you've probably never heard.

Around four thousand years ago, a group of people set out from the island of Taiwan and did something almost unbelievable. They sailed out across the open ocean — farther than anyone in history — and they did it without a map, without a compass, without any of the tools you'd think you would need.

So how did they not get hopelessly lost?

They read the world around them like a book. They watched the stars, and learned that certain stars rose in the sky right above certain islands. They felt the way the ocean waves bumped against their boats, because waves bend around islands in patterns you can actually learn. They followed seabirds flying home at sunset, because those birds sleep on land. They even watched the color of the clouds — because clouds floating over a green island can glow a little differently.

That's not luck. That's science — passed down from parents to children in songs and stories, for hundreds of years.

And their boats were genius, too. They built canoes with an extra float attached to the side — called an outrigger — so the boat wouldn't tip over in big waves. Some joined two boats together, like the world's very first catamarans. In these, they crossed thousands of miles of empty sea.

We call these people the Austronesians. And listen to how far they got. To the west, they reached Madagascar, a giant island right next to Africa. To the east, they reached Rapa Nui — Easter Island — one of the loneliest places on the whole planet.

That's more than half the width of the entire Earth. It is the biggest sea journey any group of people has ever made.

And their story still isn't over. The languages they spoke slowly grew into more than a thousand languages that are still spoken today — by around four hundred million people. If you've ever heard of Malay, Filipino, or Hawaiian, all of those trace back to those brave sailors and their clever little boats with the float on the side.

## SEGMENT 5 — THE CITY THE JUNGLE ATE

Our last stop is on land — the mainland of Southeast Asia — and it's a big one. Because right here, in the jungles of Cambodia, once stood the biggest city in the entire world.

Its name was Angkor.

Around eight hundred years ago, while most cities in Europe were still small and muddy, Angkor was enormous. As many as seven hundred and fifty thousand people may have lived there. Compare that to London at the very same time, which had maybe twenty thousand people. Angkor was in a completely different league.

So how do you feed and water a city that big? The Khmer people who built it were brilliant engineers. They dug giant reservoirs and long canals to catch the rain and move water all across the city. These waterworks were so huge that, even today, you can still see their outlines from an airplane.

And at the heart of it all, they built something breathtaking: Angkor Wat. It's a temple made of stone, and it is *still* the largest religious building on the entire planet. Its walls are covered in carvings that stretch on for miles — gods, armies, dancers, and stories from long ago. It took about thirty years and thousands of workers to build. And it is still standing today, nine hundred years later.

So what happened to this amazing city? Slowly, things went wrong. The weather turned harsh — long droughts, and then heavy floods that battered the water system the city needed to survive. Wars with neighbors didn't help. Over time, more and more people moved away.

And with no one left to hold it back, the jungle crept in. Roots cracked the stone. Vines wrapped around the towers. Whole trees grew right on top of the temples. The greatest city on Earth slowly disappeared under a thick blanket of green — until it almost looked like it had never been there at all.

But it had. And Angkor Wat is still standing right now, waiting quietly, in the middle of the forest.

## OUTRO

So there you have it. A drowned land under the sea. The oldest story ever painted. Tiny humans who lived in their own little world. Sailors who crossed half the planet by reading the stars. And a giant city that the jungle ate.

All of this is the beginning of Southeast Asia — one of the most amazing corners of the world, with a history that most people have never even heard.

And the truth is, we are still just getting started. Scientists find something new here almost every single year. So who knows? The next great discovery might be waiting under that warm blue sea… right now.

## FACT-CHECK LOG (verified against live sources, 2026-07-20)

**S1 — Sundaland**
- At the Last Glacial Maximum (~20,000 years ago) global sea level was ~120 m lower than today → "more than a hundred meters" ✓. (120 m ≈ ~40 storeys; "taller than a thirty-story building" is a safe understatement.)
- Sundaland was a single exposed landmass joining the Malay Peninsula with Sumatra, Borneo & Java; exposed land was roughly double today's → "about twice the size" ✓.
- Progressively drowned by post-glacial sea-level rise (meltwater pulses 1A ~14.5k, 1B ~11.5k ya); largely submerged between ~14,000 and ~7,000 years ago → phrasing ✓. Sources: *Communications Biology* (Nature) 2023 on Sundaland/sea-level migration; standard LGM sea-level literature.

**S2 — Sulawesi cave art**
- Leang Karampuang (Maros-Pangkep, Sulawesi): scene of a pig + three human-like figures dated to **at least 51,200 years** via laser-ablation U-series (Oktaviana et al., *Nature*, 2024) → "at least fifty-one thousand years old," "oldest known artwork," "oldest known story/narrative scene" ✓. (Prior record: 45.5 ka warty-pig at Leang Tedongnge.)

**S3 — Flores / Homo floresiensis**
- LB1 found 2003, Liang Bua; adult ~1 m tall; brain ~small (grapefruit-sized comparison is fair, ~400 cc) ✓.
- Named *Homo floresiensis*, nicknamed "the Hobbit" ✓.
- Island dwarfism; dwarf *Stegodon* (dwarf elephant relative) on Flores hunted with stone tools; "almost as small as a cow" is a fair simplification ✓.
- Disappeared from Liang Bua ~50,000 years ago; leading cause = environmental/climate change (aridification), NOT competition with us ✓.
- *H. sapiens* reached the site only ~46,000 years ago → "we showed up too late to meet them" ✓ (no proven overlap at the site). Stone tools on Flores go back ~700k yrs (Mata Menge) → "hundreds of thousands of years" ✓.

**S4 — Austronesian expansion**
- Out of Taiwan ~4,000 years ago (BP); double-hull/outrigger canoes; wayfinding by stars, ocean swells, bird flight, cloud/lagoon cues ✓.
- Spread from Madagascar (west) to Rapa Nui/Easter Island (east) — the largest pre-modern maritime expansion, >half the globe in longitude ✓.
- Austronesian language family ≈ 1,200+ languages, ~386 million speakers → "more than a thousand languages," "around four hundred million people" ✓. Malay, Filipino (Tagalog), Hawaiian are Austronesian ✓.

**S5 — Angkor**
- Peak 12th–13th century (~800 years ago); Greater Angkor the largest city of the pre-industrial world; population estimates up to ~700,000–900,000 (LiDAR; Evans et al., *PNAS/Science Advances*) → "as many as seven hundred and fifty thousand" sits safely inside the range ✓.
- London ~20,000 in the 12th century ✓.
- Angkor Wat = largest religious monument on Earth; built under Suryavarman II; ~30-year build; dedicated originally to Vishnu ✓ (faith detail omitted here to keep it simple/neutral for kids).
- Vast hydraulic system (barays, canals) whose outlines are still traceable from the air/aerial imagery ✓.
- Decline linked to climate extremes (severe droughts then intense monsoons that damaged the water network) plus warfare; capital largely abandoned by the 15th century ✓.

### Softened / kept-honest wording
- "may have lived," "as many as," "scientists think," "at least" used on every uncertain figure.
- No claim that we killed the Hobbits (evidence points to climate).
- "oldest *known*" art/story (leaves room for future finds).
