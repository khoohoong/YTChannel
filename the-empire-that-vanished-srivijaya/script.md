## VO-A — HOOK + THE CHOKE POINT (~260 words · est. ~1:55)

Right now, in a muddy brown river in Indonesia, fishermen are diving into water so dark they
work entirely by touch. And they are coming up with gold.

Rings. Beads. Temple bells. Bronze figures of the Buddha. The jewelled sword-hilts of kings.

They are pulling up the wreckage of an empire. An empire that dominated Southeast Asia for six
hundred years — as long as the distance between us and the Middle Ages — and then vanished so
completely that the world forgot it had ever existed. No ruins. No chronicles. Not even a name.

Until 1918, when one scholar, reading forgotten stones, realized that the history of an entire
region had a hole in it. A hole the size of an empire.

This is the story of Srivijaya. How it rose. Why it disappeared. And how, right now, a river is
finally giving it back.

To understand how an empire could rise here at all, look at the map. Between the Malay
Peninsula and the island of Sumatra runs a channel of water: the Strait of Malacca. In the age
of sail, this was the only practical sea road between China and everything west of it — India,
Arabia, and beyond. Silk went one way. Spices, ivory and incense went the other.

And there was no way around. Every ship, every cargo, every fortune had to pass through a
corridor that, at its narrowest, forces the ships of the world into almost single file.

Now imagine you own the toll gate.

*(re-hook)* In the seventh century, someone did.

## VO-B — THE FLOATING EMPIRE (~250 words · est. ~1:50)

On the swampy banks of the Musi River in Sumatra, a kingdom arose whose Sanskrit name announced
its ambition: Srivijaya — "Glorious Victory."

Its capital, near modern Palembang, was unlike any other imperial city. No great walls. No
stone towers. A city of wood and water — houses on stilts, markets on rafts, palaces of timber.
Its people were descendants of the Austronesian sailors from our last film — the greatest
navigators of the ancient world. Their fortress was the sea itself, patrolled by a navy that
made the strait safe for friends and lethal for rivals.

And here was the masterstroke: the monsoon. Sailing east or west, traders had to wait — for
months — for the winds to reverse. Srivijaya's harbours became the world's waiting room. Every
merchant paid, rested, and traded there, because the wind gave them no choice.

The wealth became legendary. Indian texts called Sumatra *Suvarnadvipa* — the Island of Gold.
In the year 671, a Chinese monk named Yijing stopped there on his way to India, and stayed six
months. He found more than a thousand Buddhist scholars in residence, and sent word back to
China: don't sail past this place — study here first. This swamp city had become one of the
great centres of learning on Earth.

*(re-hook)* But a capital built of wood and water has one fatal property. Stone remembers.
Wood forgets. And far across the ocean, someone was about to test how strong this empire
really was.

## VO-C — THE ENEMY FROM THE WEST (~200 words · est. ~1:30)

For three hundred years, the arrangement held. Pay Srivijaya, and the sea road stays open.

But by the eleventh century, a superpower had risen on the far side of the Bay of Bengal: the
Chola empire of southern India — master shipbuilders, with an emperor, Rajendra the First, who
had already pushed his armies to the Ganges. To the Cholas and the merchants they backed,
Srivijaya's tolls no longer looked like protection. They looked like a stranglehold.

In 1025, Rajendra did something no one believed possible. He sent a war fleet more than two
thousand kilometres across open ocean — one of the most audacious naval strikes of the entire
medieval world — and hit more than a dozen Srivijayan ports in a single campaign. The harbours
burned. The treasury was plundered. Chronicles say even the king was taken.

Srivijaya survived — in name. But every trader from Cairo to Canton now knew the truth: the
toll-keeper of the East could be beaten at sea. In its own harbours.

*(re-hook)* Empires can recover from a defeat. What they cannot recover from is irrelevance.
And the sea itself was about to turn on Srivijaya.

## VO-D — THE VANISHING (~200 words · est. ~1:30)

Empires usually die loudly — a last battle, a burning capital, a date for the history books.
Srivijaya didn't fall. It dissolved.

After the Chola raids, the spell was broken. Rival ports rose. Trade found new channels. The
centre of power drifted away from Palembang, and city by city, the network that called itself
Srivijaya quietly stopped answering to anyone.

Then came the final blows. In 1377, the Javanese empire of Majapahit — the subject of this
series' finale — sacked Palembang. And the river delivered the last insult: silt. Year by
year, the Musi's estuary crept seaward, until the great harbour city was no longer truly on
the sea at all.

And now the wooden empire paid the price of wood. Think about what you'd find if Rome had been
built of timber and rafts. Angkor left towers. Rome left a Colosseum. Srivijaya's palaces
rotted, its rafts drifted apart, its libraries fed the river mud. Within a few generations,
even the people living on the Musi no longer knew what had stood there.

*(re-hook)* The empire wasn't just gone. It was erased. And it stayed erased for six hundred
years — until a man in an archive noticed that several mysteries had the same answer.

## VO-E — THE RESURRECTION + CTA (~250 words · est. ~1:50)

The clues had been sitting in plain sight for centuries — they just lived in different worlds.

Chinese imperial records spoke of a rich tributary kingdom in the southern seas. Ancient stones
found in Sumatra carried royal proclamations in Old Malay, naming a realm no historian could
place. Indian texts praised a golden island of a thousand monks.

In 1918, the French scholar George Cœdès put the fragments side by side — and made the case
that stunned the field: these weren't different places. They were all the same forgotten
empire. Srivijaya wasn't legend. It was real, it was vast, and it had ruled the crossroads of
Asia for six centuries while leaving barely a stone behind.

And then, nearly a century later, the river began to talk. Since around 2011, dredgers and
divers in the Musi have been surfacing with rings, coins, beads, bronze bells — even gold. The
city of wood is gone forever. But the treasure of the Island of Gold is real, and it's coming
up out of the dark, piece by piece, right now.

So here's the question we can't stop thinking about — and we want your answer in the comments:
if an empire this size could vanish from memory for six hundred years… how much of human
history is simply *missing*?

Because Srivijaya isn't even the strangest case. Next time: a kingdom that lasted more than
sixteen hundred years — longer than Rome — and was deleted from the map while the modern world
watched. Subscribe, so you don't miss it.
