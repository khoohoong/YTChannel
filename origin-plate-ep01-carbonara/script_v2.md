## HOOK (≈0:00–0:25)
Carbonara is Italy's most sacred pasta.
Eggs. Guanciale. Pecorino. Black pepper. And never… ever… cream.
Italians will tell you it's ancient. Older than their grandmothers.
But here's the problem.
Before nineteen-fifty, there is no record of carbonara. Anywhere.
So who really invented it?
The answer involves an army… a Chicago guidebook called Vittles and Vice… and a historian half of Italy got furious with.

## ONCE UPON A TIME — the world before carbonara (≈0:25–1:45)
Meet Luca Cesari. He's a food historian from Bologna.
He wanted to know where Italy's famous pastas really came from.
So he went looking the hard way. One old cookbook at a time.
And the first thing he found was this.
Italians had been mixing eggs and cheese into pasta for a very long time.
In the late eighteen-thirties, a Neapolitan nobleman named Ippolito Cavalcanti published a recipe.
Pasta, tossed with lard, beaten eggs, and cheese.
Sound familiar?
Meanwhile, in the hills around Rome, cooks made pasta alla gricia.
Cured pork jowl. Pecorino. Black pepper.
Add tomato, and gricia becomes amatriciana.
Add egg… and you'd have something very close to carbonara.
All the pieces were there. For more than a hundred years.
And yet, Cesari found something strange.
Nobody called anything "carbonara."
Not one recipe. Not one menu. Not one cookbook.

## THE LEGENDS — fast (≈1:45–2:25)
Of course, Italians have stories to explain the name.
Story one. "Carbonaro" means charcoal maker.
So, the legend goes, charcoal burners in the mountains invented it over their fires.
Story two. A secret revolutionary society in the eighteen-hundreds, called the Carbonari, named it.
Story three. The black pepper looks like specks of coal dust.
They're lovely stories.
Not one of them comes with a recipe.
So if carbonara wasn't in the old cookbooks… where did it come from?

## UNTIL ONE DAY — June 1944 (≈2:25–3:30)
June, nineteen forty-four.
Allied troops roll into Rome.
And with them comes food the city hasn't seen in years.
American army supplies.
Two things in those crates were very American indeed.
Bacon. And eggs — often powdered.
To an American, that's breakfast.
To Italians at the time, bacon and eggs on pasta was not an obvious idea.
Now picture a Roman cook.
Hungry soldiers at the door. A pot of pasta on the stove.
And a pile of bacon and eggs.
Cesari thinks this is most likely where carbonara was born.
An Italian technique… built around American ingredients.

## BECAUSE OF THAT — the paper trail (≈3:30–5:00)
There's a famous story about a wartime cook in Riccione, on the Adriatic coast.
He said he served the very first carbonara to Allied officers in nineteen forty-four.
But when he wrote his life story, the dinner he described didn't include carbonara at all.
So for real evidence, we have to wait.
The twenty-sixth of July, nineteen-fifty.
A Turin newspaper, La Stampa, writes about Trastevere, in Rome.
It mentions a local restaurant.
Years earlier, it says, American officers came there looking for… spaghetti alla carbonara.
American officers. Not Roman grandmothers.
The name had arrived. And it was travelling.
Because the first published recipe wasn't printed in Italy.
It was printed in Chicago.
Nineteen fifty-two. A guidebook to the restaurants of Chicago's Near North Side.
Its name? Vittles and Vice.
Inside is a carbonara recipe from an Italian restaurant called Armando's.
Its owners came from Lucca, in Tuscany.
Their version? Bacon. Parmesan. Eggs.

## BECAUSE OF THAT — the recipe Italy forgot (≈5:00–6:15)
Italy caught up two years later.
August, nineteen fifty-four. The magazine La Cucina Italiana prints a carbonara recipe.
And it's… not what you'd expect.
Pancetta, not guanciale. A clove of garlic. And Gruyère — a Swiss cheese.
Now, fast forward to December, twenty twenty-three.
Luca Cesari cooks that exact nineteen fifty-four recipe. On camera.
The video passes one point nine million views.
And Italy is furious.
The comments fill up with outrage.
How dare he put garlic in carbonara?
Cesari's answer was simple.
"I simply remade the 1954 carbonara… It's not my fault if that was the carbonara recipe!"

## BECAUSE OF THAT — carbonara goes global (≈6:15–7:00)
That same year, nineteen fifty-four, British food writer Elizabeth David put carbonara in her book, Italian Food.
Abroad, it did what popular dishes do.
It got richer. Creamier. Easier.
And the world fell in love.
In the United States, search data now ranks it among the country's three favourite pastas.
Since twenty seventeen, it even has its own holiday. Carbonara Day. Every sixth of April.

## UNTIL FINALLY — the rules arrive (≈7:00–7:40)
Back in Italy, the recipe kept changing.
Guanciale first shows up around nineteen-sixty.
Pecorino starts pushing out Parmesan in the mid-sixties.
And cream? It stayed in many Italian recipes for decades.
It only fell out of favour in the nineteen-nineties.
That's when the strict version finally settled.
Egg. Guanciale. Pecorino. Pepper.
The "ancient" recipe… is about thirty years old.

## EVER SINCE THEN — the fight (≈7:40–8:10)
Today, Italy defends it fiercely.
In March twenty twenty-three, another historian, Alberto Grandi, told the Financial Times that carbonara was essentially an American dish.
That same month, Italy had applied to UNESCO to honour its cuisine.
The country's biggest farmers' association called Grandi's claims an attack on Italian cuisine.
In twenty twenty-five, UNESCO said yes.
So… who's right?

## VERDICT (≈8:10–8:35)
Probably both.
The technique is deeply Italian.
But carbonara itself most likely took shape after nineteen forty-four… when American supplies met Italian cooks.
As Italian food writer Eleonora Cozzella put it: Italian genius… and American resources.
So, carbonara isn't ancient. It's younger than your grandparents.
And honestly? It's still delicious.
This has been Origin Plate.
