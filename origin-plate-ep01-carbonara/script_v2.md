## HOOK (≈0:00–0:25)
Carbonara is Italy's most sacred pasta.
Eggs. Guanciale. Pecorino. Black pepper. And never… ever… cream.
Italians will tell you it's ancient. Older than their grandmothers.
But here's the problem.
Before nineteen-fifty, there is no record of carbonara. Anywhere.
So who really invented it?
The answer involves an army… a Chicago restaurant guide… and a historian half of Italy got furious with.

## ONCE UPON A TIME — the world before carbonara (≈0:25–1:35)
Meet Luca Cesari. He's a food historian from Bologna.
And he went looking for carbonara the hard way. One old cookbook at a time.
Here's what he found.
Italians had been mixing eggs and cheese into pasta for a long time.
In the late eighteen-thirties, a Neapolitan nobleman named Ippolito Cavalcanti published a recipe.
Pasta, tossed with lard, beaten eggs, and cheese.
And in the hills around Rome, cooks made pasta alla gricia.
Cured pork jowl. Pecorino. Black pepper.
Every ingredient carbonara needs… almost.
Because for the next hundred years, Cesari found something strange.
Nobody called anything "carbonara."

## THE LEGENDS — fast (≈1:35–2:05)
Of course, you've heard the legends.
Charcoal burners, cooking in the mountains.
A secret society called the Carbonari.
Black pepper that looks like coal dust.
They're lovely stories.
Not one of them has a recipe to back it up.

## UNTIL ONE DAY — June 1944 (≈2:05–3:05)
June, nineteen forty-four.
Allied troops roll into Rome.
And with them comes food the city hasn't seen in years.
American army supplies.
Two things in those crates were very American indeed.
Bacon. And eggs — often powdered.
Now picture a Roman cook.
Hungry soldiers at the door. A pot of pasta on the stove.
And a pile of bacon and eggs.
Cesari thinks this is most likely where carbonara was born.
An Italian technique… built around American ingredients.

## BECAUSE OF THAT — the paper trail (≈3:05–4:40)
And what about the famous story of a wartime cook in Riccione?
He said he served the first carbonara to Allied officers in nineteen forty-four.
But when that cook wrote his life story, the dinner he described didn't include carbonara at all.
So for the first real evidence, we have to wait.
The twenty-sixth of July, nineteen-fifty.
A Turin newspaper, La Stampa, writes about Trastevere, in Rome.
It mentions a local restaurant.
Years earlier, it says, American officers came there looking for… spaghetti alla carbonara.
American officers. Not Roman grandmothers.
Because of that, the name starts to spread.
And the first published recipe?
It wasn't printed in Italy.
It was printed in Chicago.
Nineteen fifty-two. A guide to restaurants on Chicago's Near North Side.
Inside: a carbonara recipe from an Italian restaurant called Armando's.

## BECAUSE OF THAT — the recipe Italy forgot (≈4:40–6:00)
Italy caught up two years later.
August, nineteen fifty-four. The magazine La Cucina Italiana prints a carbonara recipe.
And it's… not what you'd expect.
Pancetta, not guanciale. A clove of garlic. And Gruyère — a Swiss cheese.
Fast forward to December, twenty twenty-three.
Luca Cesari cooks that exact nineteen fifty-four recipe. On camera.
The video passes one point nine million views.
And Italy is furious.
The comments are full of outrage.
Cesari's answer was simple.
"I simply remade the 1954 carbonara… It's not my fault if that was the carbonara recipe!"

## BECAUSE OF THAT — carbonara goes global (≈6:00–6:45)
That same year, nineteen fifty-four, British food writer Elizabeth David put carbonara in her book, Italian Food.
Abroad, it did what popular dishes do.
It got richer. Creamier. Easier.
And the world fell in love with it.

## UNTIL FINALLY — the rules arrive (≈6:45–7:25)
Back in Italy, the recipe kept changing.
Guanciale first shows up around nineteen-sixty.
Pecorino starts pushing out Parmesan in the mid-sixties.
And cream? It stayed in many Italian recipes for decades.
It only fell out of favour in the nineteen-nineties.
That's when the strict version finally settled.
Egg. Guanciale. Pecorino. Pepper.
The "ancient" recipe… is about thirty years old.

## EVER SINCE THEN — the fight (≈7:25–8:00)
Today, Italy defends it fiercely.
In twenty twenty-three, another historian, Alberto Grandi, called carbonara an American dish.
Italy's biggest farmers' association called it an attack on Italian cuisine.
In twenty twenty-five, UNESCO recognised Italian cuisine as part of humanity's cultural heritage.
So… who's right?

## VERDICT (≈8:00–8:20)
Probably both.
The technique is deeply Italian.
But carbonara itself most likely took shape after nineteen forty-four… when American supplies met Italian cooks.
As Italian food writer Eleonora Cozzella put it: Italian genius… and American resources.
Carbonara isn't ancient. It's younger than your grandparents.
And honestly? It's still delicious.
