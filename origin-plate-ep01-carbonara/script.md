## COLD OPEN — THE MYTH (≈0:00–0:40)

Ask anyone in Rome, and they'll tell you: carbonara is sacred. Eggs. Guanciale. Pecorino. Black pepper. Nothing else — and heaven help you if you add cream.

In 2025, UNESCO recognised Italian cuisine itself as part of the cultural heritage of humanity. And carbonara is one of its proudest flags. A dish so old, so Roman, it surely goes back to the shepherds… or the emperors.

Except — when historians went looking for carbonara in old cookbooks, they found something strange.

Nothing.

No carbonara in the cookbooks of the eighteen-hundreds. Not a mention between the wars. As far as anyone has found, the name doesn't appear in print until nineteen-fifty.

So where did Italy's most fought-over pasta really come from? The answer involves charcoal burners, a secret society, a Chicago restaurant guide… and a lot of American bacon.

## SEGMENT 1 — THE LEGENDS (≈0:40–2:40)

Let's start with the stories everyone tells.

Legend number one: the charcoal burners. "Carbonaro" means a charcoal maker in Italian. The story goes that woodsmen in the Apennine mountains, cooking over their fires for weeks at a time, made a simple pasta from what they could carry — cured pork, hard cheese, and eggs. The black pepper on top? That's the coal dust.

It's a lovely image. It's also almost impossible to prove. Nobody has found a written recipe from those mountains.

Legend number two: the Carbonari — a secret revolutionary society in nineteenth-century Italy. Some say the dish was named in their honour. But again, there's no paper trail linking the society to a plate of pasta.

Legend number three is simpler. The name just describes the look — flecks of pepper like specks of charcoal. That might be part of the truth. But it doesn't tell us when the dish was born.

And here's the thing: there *is* a real ancestor. In the late eighteen-thirties, a Neapolitan nobleman named Ippolito Cavalcanti published a cookbook with a recipe for pasta tossed with lard, beaten eggs and cheese. Eggs and cheese on pasta were nothing new in southern Italy.

And in the countryside around Rome, cooks were already making pasta alla gricia — pasta with cured pork jowl, pecorino and black pepper. Nobody knows exactly how old it is, but it's carbonara's closest cousin. Carbonara's family tree has deep Italian roots.

But an ancestor isn't the dish. Pasta with eggs and cheese is not carbonara — just like bread and meat isn't a hamburger. To find carbonara itself, we need a date. And for that, we need to go to war.

## SEGMENT 2 — THE SOLDIERS (≈2:40–4:50)

June, nineteen forty-four. Allied troops enter Rome. For the first time in years, the city is flooded with food it hasn't seen — American army supplies. And two of the most common things in those supplies were very American indeed.

Bacon. And eggs — often powdered.

Bacon and eggs: the classic American breakfast. To Italians at the time, that pairing on pasta was not an obvious idea. To hungry Romans cooking for homesick soldiers, it may have been an inspired one.

Food historian Luca Cesari, who has traced carbonara's history recipe by recipe, believes the dish was most likely born right here, in Rome, in those years — an Italian technique, built around American ingredients.

There's a competing story too. In September nineteen forty-four, a young cook from Bologna named Renato Gualandi said he was hired to make lunch for British and American officers in the newly liberated seaside town of Riccione. With their rations — bacon, cream, cheese, powdered egg yolks — he says he made a pasta, and finished it with black pepper.

It's a great story. But it rests on Gualandi's own memory, told decades later. No one has been able to confirm it.

What we *can* confirm is the first time the word appears in print.

The twenty-sixth of July, nineteen-fifty. The Turin newspaper La Stampa runs a story about Trastevere, the old working-class heart of Rome. It mentions a local restaurant that had become known, years earlier, as the place where American officers came looking for… spaghetti alla carbonara.

American officers. Not Roman grandmothers. That's the very first clue we have.

## SEGMENT 3 — THE RECIPE THAT KEPT CHANGING (≈4:50–7:30)

Here's where it gets even stranger. The first published *recipe* for carbonara didn't appear in Italy at all.

It appeared in Chicago.

In nineteen fifty-two, an American writer named Patricia Bronté published a guide to the restaurants of Chicago's Near North Side. Inside, there's a carbonara recipe from an Italian restaurant called Armando's. The first carbonara recipe on record — printed in English, in the United States.

Italy caught up two years later. In August nineteen fifty-four, the magazine La Cucina Italiana printed a carbonara recipe. And if you showed it to a Roman purist today, they might faint.

Pancetta, not guanciale. Garlic. And Gruyère — a Swiss cheese.

Over the next forty years, carbonara kept changing shape. Cesari's research found guanciale first showing up in a carbonara recipe around nineteen-sixty. Pecorino started to edge out Parmesan from the mid-sixties. And cream? For decades, cream was a normal part of many recipes — in Italy as well as abroad. It only fell out of favour in the nineteen-nineties.

That's when the "strict" version — egg, guanciale, pecorino, pepper — settled into the form Rome defends today.

So the recipe purists guard so fiercely is, in its modern form, only about thirty years old.

## SEGMENT 4 — THE FIGHT (≈7:30–8:50)

In March twenty twenty-three, an Italian food historian named Alberto Grandi told the Financial Times that carbonara was, essentially, an American dish. He pointed to that wartime story — an Italian cook, American soldiers, American ingredients.

Italy did not take it well. The country's biggest farmers' association called it an attack on Italian cuisine. A deputy prime minister weighed in. For a few days, a plate of pasta became a national argument.

So who's right?

Probably… both sides, a little. The technique — eggs and cheese tossed through hot pasta into a silky sauce — is deeply Italian, with roots going back at least to the eighteen-hundreds. But the dish we call carbonara, with bacon and eggs at its heart, almost certainly took shape in the years after nineteen forty-four, when American supplies met Italian cooks.

As the Italian food writer Eleonora Cozzella put it: it was a combination of Italian genius and American resources.

## CLOSE (≈8:50–9:20)

Carbonara isn't ancient. It's younger than your grandparents. And that doesn't make it any less Italian. Every great dish started somewhere — usually with hungry people and whatever was in the pantry.
